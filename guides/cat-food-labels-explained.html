Cat Food Labels Explained: How to Read a Cat Food Packet

Cat food packets are designed to sell, not to inform. Pictures of fresh salmon, words such as premium and natural, and claims about real meat all compete for your attention, while the information that actually matters sits in small print on the back. Learning to read a label takes five minutes and changes every food choice you make afterwards.

The ingredients list

Ingredients are listed by weight, heaviest first, before cooking. A food that lists chicken first is mostly chicken. But watch for ingredient splitting, where a maker lists rice, rice flour, and rice gluten separately so that meat appears first, even though rice dominates the recipe overall.

Named ingredients are better than vague ones. Chicken, salmon, and lamb tell you what you are buying. Meat meal, animal derivatives, and meat by-products are less clear, though they are not automatically bad. A named meat meal, such as chicken meal, is a concentrated protein source and is common in good dry foods.

Guaranteed analysis and life stage

The guaranteed analysis lists minimum protein and fat, and maximum fibre and moisture. Adult cat food should contain at least 26 percent protein on a dry matter basis, and kittens need more. Because wet food contains water, compare foods on a dry matter basis rather than by the numbers on the packet.

Look for a statement that the food is complete and balanced for a life stage, such as adult maintenance, growth, or all life stages. This statement means the food meets recognised nutritional standards. A food without it is a complementary food, not a complete diet, and should not be the main food.

Feeding guides and dates

The feeding guide gives a daily amount for a cat of a certain weight. Treat it as a starting point and adjust by watching your cat's weight, because activity and metabolism vary. If your cat is gaining weight on the recommended amount, feed a little less.

Check the best-before date and the batch code, and store dry food in a cool, dry place in a sealed container. Once opened, use wet food within two days and dry food within four to six weeks, because fats go rancid and vitamins degrade.

Marketing words to ignore

Words such as premium, gourmet, natural, and holistic have no legal meaning on pet food labels in most countries. They describe a feeling, not a standard. Grain-free does not mean low in carbohydrates, and it does not mean better; many grain-free foods replace grain with potato or peas.

Human-grade is also a marketing term, not a guarantee of quality. What matters is the nutritional statement, the ingredient quality, and whether your cat does well on the food. A food your cat eats happily, digests well, and maintains weight on is a good food, whatever the front of the packet says.

A worked example: comparing two foods

Take two adult cat foods and compare them side by side. Food A lists chicken first, then chicken meal, then rice, with 32 percent protein and a statement that it is complete and balanced for adult maintenance. Food B lists meat and animal derivatives first, with 26 percent protein and no life-stage statement. Food A is the clearer choice, because its ingredients are named and its nutritional claim is specific.

Now check the feeding guide. Food A suggests 60 grams a day for a 4 kg cat, while Food B suggests 80 grams, which means Food A is more energy-dense and will cost less per day, even if the bag costs more. This is why comparing foods by the bag price is misleading, and why the label, not the marketing, should decide.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should the first ingredient be?

A named animal protein, such as chicken, salmon or lamb. It should be first by weight, but check the whole list for ingredient splitting.

What does complete and balanced mean?

It means the food meets recognised nutritional standards for a stated life stage and can be fed as the whole diet.

Is grain-free cat food better?

Not necessarily. Grain-free foods often replace grain with potato or peas and can be higher in carbohydrates. Judge the food by its nutritional profile, not by the absence of grain.
